Understanding Wolfram's cellular automata

A cellular automaton turns a tiny local instruction into a complete evolving world. Stephen Wolfram systematically studied this elementary one-dimensional family and numbered every possible rule from 0 to 255.

What are they?

The universe is a row of cells. Each cell has only two possible states—alive (black) or dead (white)—and all cells update simultaneously in discrete time steps. Stacking those generations vertically creates the image above: space runs from left to right, while time runs from top to bottom.

How does one update work?

A cell reads three bits: its left neighbor, itself, and its right neighbor. Three binary cells produce eight possible neighborhoods. The eight boxes above show those neighborhoods; the lower digit in each box is the cell's next state.

left + center + right → next state

How is a rule constructed?

  1. Write the rule number as eight binary digits.
  2. Assign them to 111, 110, 101, 100, 011, 010, 001, and 000.
  3. Choose a single-cell or random initial row.
  4. Apply the lookup to every cell, then repeat on the new row.

Because there are eight outputs and each can be 0 or 1, exactly 28 = 256 elementary rules exist. Fixed boundaries add dead cells outside the row; periodic boundaries connect its two ends.

Why are they important?

They demonstrate emergence: simple deterministic instructions can create stable, repeating, chaotic, or unexpectedly complex structures. Rule 90 draws a Sierpiński triangle, Rule 30 produces chaotic-looking patterns, and Rule 110 is capable of universal computation. They connect dynamical systems, artificial life, complexity, and the idea that computation can arise from local interaction.
